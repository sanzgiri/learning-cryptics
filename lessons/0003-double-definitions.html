Double Definitions

Hidden words and anagrams both had a wordplay half that built the answer from raw letters. Double definitions have no wordplay at all — no indicator, no fodder to shuffle or hunt through. Instead, the whole clue is just two definitions, back to back, for two different meanings of the same word.

The recipe

Per the Best For Puzzles tutorial, this is "the simplest kind of additional hint in a cryptic clue" — a second definition standing in for the wordplay half. Two requirements make it a valid clue rather than a lazy one:

  • The two meanings must be genuinely distinct — different senses of the word, not two synonyms for the same sense. ("Sort variety" for KIND doesn't count — both halves mean the same thing. "Benevolent type" does — "benevolent" and "type" are unrelated senses of KIND.)
  • The clue must still read as plausible English — the surface sentence should make sense on its own, even though its real meaning is two unrelated hints glued together.

The giveaway: there's no anagram/hidden-word indicator anywhere, the clue is often short, and it splits cleanly into two halves that each work as a standalone definition.

Worked example

"Ignoble foundation (4)"

No indicator word here — that's the first clue this is a double definition. Split it: "Ignoble" is one definition, "foundation" is another. Both describe the same 4-letter word: BASE — a base can be "ignoble" (a base motive) and it can mean "foundation" (a base of operations). Two unrelated senses of the same word, both pointing at the answer.

Practice

Three more, sourced from the same Best For Puzzles tutorial. For each, find the split point and check both halves work as separate definitions of your answer.

"Cricketers use these flying mammals (4)"

No wordplay indicator — split the clue into two separate definitions.
One half is sporting equipment, the other half is a nocturnal animal.
Definition 1: Cricketers use these (bats, the equipment). Definition 2: flying mammals (bats, the animal). Same word, two senses: BATS.

"Yearn for quite a while (4)"

No wordplay indicator — split the clue into two separate definitions.
One half means "to long for"; the other is an old-fashioned way to say "a long stretch of time/pain".
Definition 1: Yearn for (to ache for something). Definition 2: quite a while (a long ache, a lasting pain). Same word, two senses: ACHE.

"Put up with an animal (4)"

No wordplay indicator — split the clue into two separate definitions.
One half means "to tolerate"; the other is a large animal.
Definition 1: Put up with (to bear something). Definition 2: an animal (a bear). Same word, two senses: BEAR.
