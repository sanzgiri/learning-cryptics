Lesson 1 · Cryptic Crosswords

The Anatomy of a Cryptic Clue

Your goal is to sit down with a Guardian or Times cryptic and actually solve it. Right now, cryptic clues probably look like nonsense sentences. They aren't. Every single one — no matter how strange it looks — is built from the same contract. Once you can see that contract, the nonsense turns into a puzzle you can crack.

Every clue makes two promises

A cryptic clue is made of two parts, sitting side by side in the same sentence:

  • The definition — a plain, ordinary synonym for the answer, exactly like you'd find in a quick crossword. It sits at the very start or the very end of the clue, never in the middle.
  • The wordplay — a coded recipe, built from the rest of the clue, that constructs the answer letter by letter through some trick.

Both parts independently point to the same answer. That's what makes cryptics different from a vague quick-crossword clue: you're not guessing at one fuzzy hint, you're solving two problems that have to agree. When your answer satisfies both the definition and the wordplay, you know — not guess — that you're right.

The number in brackets at the end, like (5) or (4,3), is the enumeration: how many letters the answer has, and how it splits across words. It's a hard constraint, not a hint about meaning.

The Guardian's own crossword editors put it the same way: a cryptic clue is "a definition plus a piece of wordplay, both pointing at the same answer." Worth reading in full — see the primary source linked at the bottom of this lesson.

Your first wordplay trick: hidden words

The easiest wordplay trick to spot, and the best place to start, is the hidden word. The recipe is: the answer is spelled out, letter by letter, running straight through a stretch of the clue — ignore the word breaks and just read the letters in order.

Setters flag this with an indicator word that means "contained within," such as: in, within, part of, some of, hidden in, held by, found in. When you spot one of these, go hunting for a run of consecutive letters spanning two or more words.

Worked example

"Sick in April lecture (3)"

Sick is the definition — sitting at the start, exactly where we'd expect. That leaves "in April lecture" as the wordplay. In is our hidden-word indicator, so we read straight through April lecture, ignoring the word break, hunting for a run of letters:

a - p - r - i - l - l - e - c - t - u - r - e

Hiding inside "aprILLecture" is ILL — three letters, matches the enumeration, and it means "sick." Both promises kept. That's the answer.

Practice

Three clues. Try each one before revealing — the effortful guess is what makes it stick, even (especially) when you get it wrong first.

"Not fresh, part of almost alert (5)"

Indicator is part of — hunt for a run of letters spanning the two words, ignoring the space.
Look right where "almost" ends and "alert" begins — the answer straddles that boundary.
Definition: Not fresh. Indicator: part of. Fodder: almoSTALErt → hidden inside "almoSTALErt".

"Garden figure hidden in sign omens (5)"

Indicator is hidden in — read straight through "sign omens" ignoring the space.
It starts partway through "sign" and finishes partway through "omens".
Definition: Garden figure. Indicator: hidden in. Fodder: siGNOMEns → hidden inside "siGNOMEns".

"Get up within safari search (5)"

Indicator is within — read straight through "safari search" ignoring the space.
It starts inside "safari" (near the end) and finishes inside "search" (near the start).
Definition: Get up. Indicator: within. Fodder: safARISEarch → hidden inside "safARISEarch".
