Cryptic Definitions

Double definitions gave you two separate, ordinary definitions with no wordplay at all. Cryptic definitions go a step further: there's only one definition, and it's deliberately phrased to send you down the wrong path.

The recipe

Per the Best For Puzzles tutorial, a cryptic definition merges the definition and the cryptic misdirection into one phrase — "the solver gets only one bite of the cherry." There's no separate wordplay half to fall back on if the definition doesn't click; the whole clue is the definition, just stretched or twisted so the obvious reading isn't the real one.

There's no fixed indicator word to spot here — that's the point. The giveaway is structural: no anagram/hidden/reversal-style indicator anywhere, and often (not always) a question mark at the end, warning you the surface reading is a trap.

Worked example

"A pop group? (6,4)"

The obvious first read is "a pop music group" — a red herring. "Pop" here means burst, and "group" means a band: RUBBER BAND. Nothing to shuffle or hide — just a familiar phrase read the wrong way on purpose, then the right way once you see it.

Practice

No wordplay to decode here — just look for the idiom or double meaning hiding in plain sight.

"When you hit the sack (7)"

No separate wordplay here — the whole phrase is one sly definition. Think about the idiom "hit the sack."
It's the time of day/night when that idiom applies — not a description of an actual sack.
The whole clue is the definition: "hit the sack" is an idiom for going to sleep, so "when" that happens is BEDTIME.

"Not quite the full ticket (9)"

This plays on a British idiom about someone who's "a bit odd."
"The full ticket" means being completely sane/normal — this word means falling short of that.
The whole clue is the definition: "not the full ticket" is an idiom for being a bit odd — ECCENTRIC.

"A shocking fish (3)"

"Shocking" isn't about surprise here — think literally.
A specific fish is famous for delivering an actual electric shock.
The whole clue is the definition: "shocking" reads at first as "surprising," but here it's literal — an EEL (electric eel) delivers a real shock.
