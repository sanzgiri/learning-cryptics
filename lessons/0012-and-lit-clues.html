&Lit Clues

The capstone. Every clue so far has had two separable halves: a definition, and a wordplay recipe, sitting next to each other. An &lit clue (short for "and literally so") collapses the two into one — the entire clue is simultaneously the wordplay and a plain, honest definition of the answer. There's no separate half to fall back on; both readings have to work over every word in the clue.

The recipe

Per the Best For Puzzles tutorial, &lit clues "contain no definition part" in the usual sense — instead "the clue as a whole functions as the straightforward definition of the solution," while also, in full, working as wordplay (an anagram, a charade, a hidden word — any trick you already know). Setters and solvers alike treat a good &lit with real respect: per the source, "devising a good original &lit clue is no easy task," precisely because the whole surface sentence has to be true on both levels at once.

Practically, that means: don't go looking for a separate definition word at the start or end. Read the whole clue as one description, and check whether the same words also rearrange, conceal, or concatenate into the answer.

Worked example

"Terribly evil (4)"

Read as a whole, "terribly evil" is just a plain, honest description of something VILE. Read as wordplay, "terribly" is an anagram indicator and "evil" is the fodder:

EVIL → VILE

Same four words, same four letters, doing both jobs at once. That's the whole trick.

Practice

No separate definition to find — the whole clue is doing double duty. Look for a familiar trick (charade, anagram, hidden word) hiding inside a phrase that's also just a plain description of the answer.

"Outside the law (6)"

No separate definition tail here — the whole phrase IS the definition. Also read it as two small words glued together, charade-style.
"Outside" and "the law", concatenated left to right.
Whole clue = definition: someone outside the law is an OUTLAW. Also wordplay (charade): OUT + LAW = OUTLAW.

"In doors (7)"

Again, the whole phrase doubles as the definition. Split it into two small charade pieces.
"In" and "doors", concatenated left to right.
Whole clue = definition: being "in doors" means being INDOORS. Also wordplay (charade): IN + DOORS = INDOORS.
