Cheat sheet

TypeMechanicTypical indicators
Hidden wordAnswer runs across consecutive letters in the clue, ignoring word breaks.in, within, part of, some of, hidden in, held by, found in
AnagramFodder's letters shuffle exactly into the answer.broken, mixed, chaotic, dancing, wild, reformed, acting up, oddly, at sea
Double definitionNo indicator — two genuine, distinct senses of the same word, back to back.(none — that's the giveaway)
CharadeTwo or more parts, each separately defined, concatenated left to right.often no indicator; juxtaposition, "and"
ContainerOne part split open, another part inserted inside it.holding, surrounding, swallowing, taking in / within, filling, entering, tucked into
ReversalFodder read backwards spells the answer.going round, turned, backward, reversed, overturned, sent back
SubtractionLetters removed from a fodder word leave the answer.beheaded, first off / heartless, hollow / incomplete, endless / without limits
Bits and PiecesOnly some letters of a word used — first, middle, last, or alternating.head of, heart of, end of / regularly, oddly, alternately
HomophoneFodder sounds identical to the answer, spelled differently.so we hear, heard, so it's said, in speech, we hear, by the sound of it
Cryptic definitionOne deliberately misleading definition, no separate wordplay.(none — often a trailing "?")
Complex clueTwo or more of the above tricks stacked in one clue.look for two indicators, not one
&Lit clueThe whole clue is simultaneously the definition and the wordplay.no separate definition tail; often "?" or "!"

American vs. British cryptics

  • Rules: American cryptics (rare — mainly GAMES magazine, The Nation, occasional New Yorker puzzles) hold to stricter construction rules than British ones: every word in the clue must serve the wordplay or definition (no "extra" surface-smoothing words), and a clue is generally allowed only one wordplay device — no complex clues.
  • Missing clue types: double definitions and cryptic definitions are common in British and Canadian cryptics but essentially unused in American ones.
  • Ximenean vs. libertarian: within British cryptics themselves, The Times leans Ximenean (strict grammatical precision in indicators — the wordplay must parse exactly, not just "get the message across"), while The Guardian leans libertarian (more relaxed, as long as it's clear). Named after Ximenes (Derrick Somerset Macnutt), author of the 1966 rulebook Ximenes on the Art of the Crossword Puzzle.
  • Popularity: cryptics are mainstream daily fare in Britain and common in Canada, India, and Australia, but a niche format in the US, where straight (quick-style) crosswords dominate.
